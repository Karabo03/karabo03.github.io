Accounts payable automation

Claude reads supplier invoices. Python decides whether to pay them.

Invoices arrive as PDFs in four different layouts, and about one in six is a scan with no readable text. A language model reads them; twelve controls written in plain Python then match each one against its purchase order and goods received note.

284Invoices processed
R3.33mExposure found
148Errors planted to test it
80.4%Recall on those errors
How it works
  • No model touches a payment decision. That has to come out the same every time and be explainable to an auditor, so the controls are plain code.
  • The model is instructed to transcribe and not to correct. A helpful model that quietly fixes wrong VAT would silence the control that exists to catch it.
  • Every error was planted deliberately and recorded before a single invoice was read, so precision and recall are measured rather than claimed. False alarms are counted too.

Bank customer churn analysis

Which customers are about to leave, and is it worth spending money to keep them.

Twenty four months of retail banking data for eight thousand customers. Ten SQL queries, a logistic regression that scores every customer, a Power BI dashboard, and a business case a finance manager could argue with.

8 000Customers analysed
R1.67mFee revenue at risk a year
0.716Model AUC
R3.63Returned per rand spent
What it found
  • Customers holding one product churned at 17.8 percent against 4.3 percent for those holding five. A complaint left open pushed churn to 27.0 percent against 9.3 percent for customers who never complained.
  • The training data was cut off before the outcome window so the answer could not leak into the model. The highest risk band, 760 customers, went on to churn at 31.7 percent against 7.3 percent in the lowest.
  • The business case shows a net benefit of R108 741 and a break even save rate of 4.4 percent, which is the number that decides whether the retention campaign is worth running at all.

AquaLogic — bulk water leak detection

A pipe that phones a person the moment it starts leaking, instead of waiting for someone to notice.

South Africa loses close to half the water it treats before it reaches a tap, almost all of it to pipe bursts nobody spots in time. AquaLogic is a sensor and alerting system for the one stretch of pipe where losing water costs the most: the bulk line between a treatment plant and a reservoir, water that has already been pumped and chemically cleaned before it leaks away. An ESP32 board on the pipe reads pressure and flow roughly once a second. When pressure drops and flow rises at the same time, a leak signature, the system writes an alert, then calls, texts and emails a technician on rotation until somebody answers. Built and paid for out of pocket, funded afterwards through a Samsung Bursary reimbursement claim, and live with real phone calls since 9 August 2026 at ujaqualogic.co.za.

47.3%Treated water lost unpaid nationally, 2025 No Drop report
R10.3bnJoburg's own water losses over four years, Auditor General
R6,445What the working prototype cost to build
9 AugLive with real calls, texts and emails since
How it works
  • The board only reports what it reads, once a minute a watcher checks for a leak flag, and if there is one it phones the on call administrator, trying again every five minutes until somebody acknowledges it. The pipe decides something is wrong, the server decides who gets woken, a person decides who goes out. No dashboard assumes anyone is already staring at a screen.
  • The public can report a leak from the site with no account. The system then checks that report against the sensors: both agreeing means dispatch immediately, only the public means the leak may sit past the last sensor, and only the sensors with no public report means a burst running underground where nobody can see it, which is the exact failure the project exists to catch.
  • Proven on a bench rig and in a simulator, not yet on a real bulk main. Every threshold would need to be measured again against a real pipe before anyone trusted it with real water, and that is said plainly rather than papered over.
